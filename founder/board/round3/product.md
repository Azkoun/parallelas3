# Product lens (round 3)

The Product lens asks four things: what the one thing is, what the first five minutes feel like, what to cut, and where the experience passes between people or systems. Both finalists now answer the first question. They differ on the last one.

## D2 ClaimBack Recovery: Score 6 | Vote: FUND IF

**Strong**
- It has the best one-sentence pitch in three rounds: "Send us the denials; we keep 20% of what we get back; nothing recovered, nothing owed." It needs no paragraph.
- The cuts are real. There is no integration, no setup fee and no checker at launch, and it serves one emirate with one kind of clinic. This is focus.
- The day-7 moment is good: a number per insurer, with the appeals already written. The clinic owner sees money they had written off.

**Top 3 killers**
1. **The company does not own the step that matters.** After the report, the result depends on insurers: their portals, their response times, and how willing they are to reverse a denial. The clinic hears "we found X" on day 7 and may see cash months later, or never. The best moment comes early and the disappointment comes late. Through this lens, that is the experience in the wrong order.
2. **The first five minutes ask the most of the buyer.** "A read-only export of 12 months of denials plus remittance files" assumes a small dental or physio clinic can produce that file easily. It also assumes the clinic will hand over patient-level health data to a new vendor. Open questions: how clean these exports really are, and what consent or data-hosting rules apply (/founder-consumer to test how willing clinics are; legal review is outside these skills).
3. **There are seams at the money.** Each step changes hands: the clinic's billing staff, ClaimBack's humans and AI, the insurer's portal, the remittance file, and finally an invoice for 20%. The clinic and ClaimBack have to agree on what counts as "recovered". One disputed invoice ends the relationship. Open questions: how many of the 12-month-old denials are still inside each insurer's appeal window, and how long recovery actually takes (/founder-competitors for insurer rules and what clinics complain about publicly; /founder-cfo for how cash timing hits a contingency model).

**Conditions**
- The day-7 report counts only denials that can still be appealed, so the headline number is one the company can actually chase.
- Recovered cash is matched to remittance lines automatically, and the client sees the same ledger used to calculate the fee.
- ClaimBack's own team does the export with the clinic, either on site or on a call. The clinic should never have to work out how to export.

**Single change to reach 8+:** Own the whole loop, from export to invoice matched to remittances, and promise only what is inside that loop. That means a day-7 report limited to appealable claims, plus a live recovery ledger the clinic can watch move. Then the experience gets better after day 7 instead of going quiet.

## E2 BidDesk Matrix: Score 7 | Vote: FUND IF

**Strong**
- The experience starts from a real moment and works backwards. The estimator uploads a thousand-page package at night, and the matrix and the 20 riskiest clauses are waiting in the morning. That moment is easy to picture and easy to want.
- The company owns the whole delivery. No third party sits between the upload and the result. Speed, completeness and format are all under its control, which is what this lens wants most.
- What it says no to is explicit. Proposal drafting and BOQ pricing are held back "until the matrix is trusted". That is the right order.

**Top 3 killers**
1. **"Complete" is a promise the buyer cannot check, and it is judged by experts.** Estimators know what a missed liquidated-damages clause costs. One miss on a giga-project package will undo ten good deliveries. The guarantee says "incomplete = free" but does not say how incompleteness is detected. Open question: what accuracy and recall estimators actually demand, and how they verify it today (/founder-consumer).
2. **The parts nobody sees are the hard part.** These include scanned drawings and specs, mixed Arabic and English, particular conditions that override general conditions, and amendments issued mid-tender. Delivering in 24h at that quality, overnight, is unproven. Budget, team and technical skill are not given (open question for the founder; /founder-cfo for what a human-review layer costs per tender).
3. **There are two products in the box.** The matrix and the risk-clause report are related but separate jobs. The counterparty rules library, with approved-vendor lists and owners' evaluation habits, is a third thing pulling for attention. The library is the moat, but at launch it threatens focus. Open questions: whether the contractor side is really unowned, and what tools estimators already use (/founder-competitors, flagged unverified in the brief).

**Conditions**
- Every line in the matrix cites the page and clause it came from, so the estimator can check any row in seconds. That is how trust is earned without asking for faith.
- Output goes into the format the bid team already works in, probably a spreadsheet. A new viewer they have to learn is not the product.
- The guarantee is defined precisely: a missed requirement the client flags, checked against the citation trail, means the tender is free.

**Single change to reach 8+:** Launch on one project owner's tender family only. Make the matrix verifiably complete against that owner's known contract forms, with every row cited to a page. A narrow promise the company can prove is complete beats a broad one it cannot.

## My pick

**E2 BidDesk Matrix.** Both are now focused. E2's whole experience, from upload to morning delivery, is in the company's hands, so craft can make it excellent. D2's best moment comes on day 7, and everything after that is handed to insurers the company does not control. Neither earns an 8 yet. E2 is one scoping decision away from it. D2 needs its whole back half rebuilt around a ledger it owns.
